**Question:** How do the films follow conflicts between family or close people
when fighting injustice?

## A useful comparison

Both films make a public struggle costly to people who care about one another,
but they do not frame that struggle in the same way. In *Avengers: Endgame*,
the Avengers try to reverse a catastrophe inflicted on the wider world. In
*The Godfather*, the Corleones are also a criminal organization: protecting
family can involve intimidation and violence, not simply fighting injustice.
Treating the families as morally equivalent would flatten an important
difference. [Endgame source, revision 1383904568](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avengers:_Endgame&oldid=1383904568); [The Godfather source, revision 1385766772](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Godfather&oldid=1385766772).

In *Endgame*, Natasha Romanoff sacrifices herself so Clint Barton can obtain
the Soul Stone, which the team needs for its attempt to restore the people lost
to Thanos. The source also reports screenwriter Stephen McFeely describing the Avengers as the family
Natasha wants back. This makes the cost personal: helping the wider group
requires the loss of someone within it. The reported explanation is a
filmmaker's interpretation; the plot passage itself establishes the sacrifice,
not every motive a viewer might infer.

In *The Godfather*, Michael responds to the threat against his father by
proposing and carrying out the killings of Sollozzo and McCluskey. Later, after
Sonny is murdered, **Vito**, not Michael, negotiates an end to the vendettas
in exchange for Michael's safe return, including a concession on the drug
trade. These choices show two different protective responses within one
family: escalation and compromise. The source establishes those actions and
the negotiated terms; it does not prove that either character's response is a
general fight for justice.

**Writer's takeaway:** One way to compare the films is to ask *who pays for a
protective choice*. Natasha's act costs her own life in pursuit of a collective
goal. Michael's protective violence kills others and draws him further into
family conflict; Vito later accepts a compromise to protect a surviving son.
That is an interpretive contrast grounded in the cited events, not a claim that
the films have the same moral position.

**Try it in an original scene:** A reformer can expose a corrupt institution
only with testimony from a sibling implicated in it. Write one version where
the reformer takes a personal risk to protect the sibling, and another where
the reformer protects the sibling by making an ethically troubling concession.
Let the consequences—not a speech explaining the theme—show what each choice
costs.
